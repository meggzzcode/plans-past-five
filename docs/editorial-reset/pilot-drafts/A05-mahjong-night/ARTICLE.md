## BODY

### Opening (no heading on the page: scene, then the promise)

At 6:25 on a Friday the table is clear, the lamp is on and nobody has arrived yet. By 9:30 four people will have played a few hands of American mahjong, one of them will have said "wait, how does this one work," and the snacks will have stayed well away from the tiles.

This is a host's guide to that evening: when each thing happens, how to set the table, where everyone sits, how to teach the first game without a lecture, and what to buy or skip. It is written for four players, and for a host who may be learning alongside her guests. Nothing here depends on buying anything new, if you already have a set and a card.

### The evening at a glance

A first mahjong night works best with a loose, honest schedule. This one assumes four people, a three-hour evening and at least one beginner. Move the clock, keep the order.

| Time | What happens | Host's job |
|---|---|---|
| 6:25 | Table ready, lamp on, snacks out of reach of the tiles | Check that every seat has a rack and a card |
| 6:30 | Guests arrive, drinks in hand | No mahjong talk yet; this is the warm-up |
| 6:50 | Sit down, find your seat | Show the tiles first, rules second |
| 7:00 | Teach the shape of the game (ten minutes, no more) | Use the card, not a speech |
| 7:15 | Walk through one hand with tiles face up | Slow, out loud, no scoring pressure |
| 7:45 | First real hand, played slowly | Everyone may ask; nobody is rushed |
| 8:15 | Break: refill drinks, wash hands | Check the side table, set out the second round of snacks |
| 8:30 | Two or three more hands | Let the group start to run itself |
| 9:15 | Last hand called aloud | Name it, so the evening has an end |
| 9:30 | Tidy and say goodbye | Tiles back in their case; the card goes with the host |

If this is a work-night evening, start 30 minutes earlier. If one guest is brand new to everything, protect the 7:15 walk-through; it is the part that most decides whether they come back.

### Before the night

Three small decisions, made a week ahead, save the most awkwardness on the night.

**Agree on the game.** "Mahjong" covers several rule families. This guide is about the American game, which is played with a yearly card from the National Mah Jongg League (the League). Ask each guest in your invitation whether she has played, and which version. If one guest learned another variant, say in advance that tonight is the American game and the card decides arguments.

**Decide who is bringing what.** One set, one card per player. If you do not own a set, ask a friend to bring one, or see the kit section below. Four racks are needed, one for each seat, and a set whose racks and tiles come in the same box avoids a size mismatch. Racks and tiles from different makers can differ in size, which is the reason to keep them together.

**Pick the date.** Evenings after work are common; a Sunday afternoon gives you daylight and a gentler pace. Either way, tell guests how long it runs, so a beginner is not wondering when she can leave.

### The table

You need a table that seats four with elbow room, and room beside it for something to eat from. A card table, a small dining table or a folding table all work; our research notes put the useful range at roughly 34 to 36 inches across, which is guidance, not a rule. If the table is smaller, the racks start to crowd the wall of tiles in the middle.

**Cover it.** Tiles clatter on bare wood or glass. A folded blanket, a felt cloth or a thick tablecloth quiets the sound and keeps the tiles from skating. Already own something thick? Use that. (We are not recommending a purpose-made mat yet; see the kit section.)

**Light it.** One lamp that does not cast a glare on the tiles. A lamp is also what makes the evening feel like an evening.

**Put one thing at every seat:**
- a rack (and pusher, if your set has them)
- a card, the current year's League card, one per player (shared cards cause crowding, and everyone wants to look at it constantly in the first hour)
- a coaster or a small napkin
- nothing else. Not the phone, not the glass.

**Keep the snacks off the table.** A side table, a sideboard or a tray on a nearby chair. The snacks and drinks live there and are fetched, and the tiles stay dry. More on this below.

### Seating

Four players, four sides. Put the person who knows the game best next to the person who knows it least, so questions are whispered rather than shouted across the table. If there are two beginners, seat them either side of the host.

The seat positions in mahjong are named after the four winds, and they matter for who deals first and how play moves around the table, but you do not need to teach that before the first hand. A simple line is enough: "The dealer starts, and play moves around the table in one set direction." Check the League's own rules materials for the official direction and wording rather than relying on us.

[TRIM] Choose the seat for each guest in advance and write nothing on name cards. Name cards at a four-person table read as formal.

### Teaching the first game

Most hosts over-teach. The aim at 7:00 is not to explain mahjong; it is to get four people to the point where playing a hand is easier than discussing one. This is our suggested sequence, in editorial judgement; it is not drawn from a published teaching method, and it is worth a read-through by someone who plays the American game (see ISSUES).

1. **Show the tiles (three minutes).** Pass them around. The three suits (dots, bamboo and characters), the winds, the dragons, the flowers and the jokers. Jokers are the tiles that make beginners smile; tell them they are wild in most places and not in all of them, and that the card tells you where.
2. **Show the card (three minutes).** Every hand on the card is a pattern of tile types, not a fixed set of tiles. Turn it so everyone can see how the hands are grouped. Do not read it aloud; do not reproduce hands in invitations or notes. The League prints a new card each year, and its hands change, so always use the current card, never an old one.
3. **The goal in a sentence.** "You are trying to make one of the hands on the card, using the tiles you are dealt and the ones you draw and pick up." That is the whole game at 7:05.
4. **Charleston, lightly.** The American game opens with the Charleston, a tile-passing ritual before play. For a first night, do the first round of passing and call it done, then tell the table the rest exists. This is a host's choice; confirm what you want to do against the League's materials.
5. **Walk through one hand with tiles face up (twenty minutes).** The host plays out a hand slowly, naming each decision: "I am keeping this pair because it fits this line on the card." Beginners learn more from watching a plan than from hearing a rulebook.
6. **Play a hand for real, slowly.** Anyone may ask. No scoring talk on hand one; play it for the shape of it.
7. **Call time on the teaching at 7:45.** After this, answer questions as they arise instead of explaining ahead of them.

**What to say when someone is lost.** "Hold the tiles that fit one line of the card, and let go of the rest." It is not a full strategy, but it keeps a first-timer from trying to hold six possibilities at once.

**What to do with a disagreement.** Say beforehand that the League's card and rules decide, and that you will check after the hand, not during it.

### Snacks and drinks

The brief for the food is simple: bite-sized, dry or not greasy, eaten with a pick, a napkin or one hand, and placed where nobody reaches across the table for it. Oily fingers mark paper cards and slick tiles. Hands wiped on napkins are what keep the game clean. We are not making any claim about how a specific set's finish behaves.

**Easy things to put out**
- cheese cubes with picks, or a small wedge with a knife on the side table
- crackers, breadsticks or plain pretzels
- grapes, clementine segments, apple slices with a little lemon
- nuts, olives or roasted chickpeas in a small bowl with a spoon
- one sweet thing that does not need a plate: small shortbread, dark chocolate squares

**A second round at the break.** Keep one thing back for 8:15 (a warm item such as a small baked savoury, or a bowl of something fresh) so the break has a moment.

**Drinks.** Lead with tea, coffee, sparkling water, a pitcher of lemonade or iced tea, and a hot drink for the colder months. If guests like wine or a cocktail, that is fine as an extra, not the centre of the evening. Our reasoning: counting and recalling the card takes attention, and a first-time player needs it. Lidded cups or glasses with a coaster live on the side table, never on the playing surface.

**The one rule that keeps the table clear:** "Drinks and food stay on the side table; hands wiped before you pick up a tile." Say it once, kindly, at 6:50.

### House etiquette (a card you can read aloud)

Short enough to say in a minute, and gentle enough for a first night.

- The card decides. Check it after the hand, not during.
- Hold your tiles where only you can see them; no peeking, no commentary on someone else's rack.
- Say what you are doing out loud when you pick up a discard; the table needs to hear it.
- No hovering and no hinting on a stranger's rack. A beginner may ask the host for help; anyone else waits to be asked.
- Phones face-down on the side table.
- Mistakes are normal tonight. Fix and carry on.

### What beginners get wrong

1. **Starting without the card.** Every beginner expects to remember the hands. Nobody does. The card is on the table for the whole game.
2. **Using an old card.** The League issues a new card every year and the hands change. An old card quietly teaches the wrong hands.
3. **Teaching too much too soon.** Four rules beat twenty. Teach the shape, then play.
4. **Mixing sets.** Racks and tiles from different makers can disagree on size. Keep the set together in one box.
5. **Treating "mahjong" as one game.** Agree on the family of rules before anyone sits.
6. **Food on the table.** The tiles do not enjoy it and neither does the card.
7. **No ending.** Without a named last hand the evening drifts. Call it at 9:15.

### Questions

**Do I need to know the game before I host?**
Not fully. You need to be able to deal, explain the aim of the game and follow the card. Learning slightly ahead of your guests is a fine place to start.

**Which year's card should we use?**
The current one. The League issues a new card each year and the hands change. Check the League's site for when the new card goes on sale.

**Can I use any set?**
For the American game you need the right tile mix: according to our research notes, at least eight jokers and eight flowers. If a listing does not state the contents, treat it as unconfirmed.

**What if someone plays a different version?**
Agree in your invitation that the evening is the American game with the League card. Differences are fine at another table, on another night.

**How long does a first hand take?**
Longer than you think. Plan the evening so the first real hand runs slowly and the group gets faster by itself.
