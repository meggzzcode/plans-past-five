# M4 - Articles, second half (alphabetical): marketing and sell-appeal review

Scope: the 14 published articles from `how-to-host-a-paint-night-at-home` to `things-to-do-on-the-weekend`, plus the 7 draft titles and deks. I read the full source text of all 14 and the live meta tags, and I viewed screenshots of the paint night (desktop), solo Saturday (mobile) and after-work (full-page desktop) pages. I did not edit any site files.

## 1. Verdict and sell-appeal scores

The writing is warm, specific and honest. The structure is strong: numbered ideas, first-30-minutes boxes, "what you need" lists and a clean "Shop your hobby" block at the end. The weak point is selling. Almost every article does the opposite of a marketer's job in the first 100 words. It either leads with problems ("most picnics go wrong", "book clubs die of admin gaps", "craft aisles make everything look necessary") or with explanation ("a junk journal is what you get when..."). It rarely leads with the desire: the picture of her having the evening, Saturday or hobby, and feeling something. Shopping appears last, in a block titled "Optional" with the dek "Start with what you have." The anti-hard-sell posture is good for trust, but it is applied so uniformly that the Amazon block reads as an apology. Honest persuasion would still say what each item does for her evening. The listicles (solo dates, weekend, after work, low-energy) have excellent hooks and are the most Pinterest-ready. The how-to guides are useful but sound like manuals. The titles are mostly strong. Several have no hero-matched shopping moment, and three pages share one stock hero image (`junkJournal`), which dilutes visual desire.

## 7. Cross-page consistency issues

1. Intro pattern: 8 of 14 open with a problem or a failure list ("go wrong", "die of", "tends to disappear", "almost none of it is"). The brand is meant to be a delighted friend. Rebalance toward the desire first, then the fix.
2. "Optional." appears in at least six shop deks ("Optional. Start with what you have", "Optional. Play first, buy later" and similar) and in several essentials/optional lists. Repetition makes the shop block feel apologetic and template-like.
3. Anchor/treat/rest (weekend, three-part) versus anchor/treat/wander/rest (solo Saturday, four-part). Cross-link them explicitly so they read as one system.
4. Heading style: some H2s are numbered steps ("Step 1:"), some are plain, some are "If you..." mood headers. The mood headers (after work, weekend) are the strongest. Consider using them as a house format for the listicles.
5. Tense and voice: most of the set uses "you". Paint night uses "the host" and "guests". Both are fine, but pick one dominant voice per page type (host guides can say "you" for the host).
6. "Era" and "girls" usage: "Pickleball Era" and the planned "Air-Dry Clay Era" lean on the same joke. Fine, but do not extend it beyond two titles. "Aesthetic Picnic" is similar. Keep tone moderate.
7. Cost labels vary: "Free", "Often free", "Varies", "Under $25", "Under $25 to start", "Free-$20", "Groceries you'd buy anyway", "Pantry staples". Standardize to Free / Low / Varies, or make sure any dollar figure is clearly "usually", since the business rules forbid invented prices.
8. Final CTA: closing paragraphs are always "See [x] or [y]". Almost no article ends on one action. Pick one per page (Make Some Plans, Hobby Finder or Save to My Plans).
9. Byline: "BY THE PLANS PAST FIVE EDITORS" is consistent and safe. Keep.
10. Disclosure: the body copy is careful about honesty ("Prices vary", "check what is included"). Confirm the Amazon disclosure sits close to every shop block, since the button wording is being changed.
